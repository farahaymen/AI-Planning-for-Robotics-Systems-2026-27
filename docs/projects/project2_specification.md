# Project 2: The Autonomous Robotics Grand Challenge

**Weight** 30 percent academic, 10 percent competition performance, the academic component multiplied by your individual peer contribution factor
**Teams** 3 or 4 students, same teams as Project 1 unless agreed otherwise
**Issued** Week 9, with the arena grammar and the scoring function
**Competition** Week 12, as a single event
**Report deadline** End of week 12

---

## 1. The mission

Your robot is placed at an unknown pose in an arena it has not seen. It must
visit four checkpoints in sequence, avoiding static and dynamic obstacles, and
finish within the time limit. One route will be blocked. Nobody may touch the
robot or its software once the run has started.

The arena is unseen, but it is not arbitrary. It is drawn from the published
grammar in section 3, which you have been evaluating against all term, and the
seed is drawn publicly at the start of the session.

## 2. What you may build

Any autonomy approach you can justify technically:

- Nav2 as configured in Lab 6, tuned
- Nav2 with a modified or replaced global planner
- Nav2 with a learned local controller in place of DWB or MPPI
- A hybrid architecture in the style of Lab 10
- Something else, if you can defend it in the report

You are not required to use reinforcement learning. A well tuned classical stack
with good recovery behaviour is a legitimate and competitive entry, and the Lab
10 measurements suggest it is the sensible starting point. What is not acceptable
is an unexplained choice: the report has to say why you built what you built.

**Read the Lab 10 results before deciding.** On thirty unseen arenas from this
grammar, a purely reactive classical controller reached 6.7 percent success and a
purely learned policy reached 0 percent. Four checkpoints through a partitioned
arena needs a global planner. That is a measurement, not an opinion.

## 3. The arena grammar

Every scored arena is generated by `arc_rl/arenas.py` from this grammar, and a
single integer seed reproduces it exactly.

| Parameter | Range |
|-----------|-------|
| Arena footprint | 10 to 14 m square |
| Internal partitions | 2 to 4, each with one doorway |
| Doorway width | 0.9 to 1.6 m |
| Static obstacles | 8 to 16, radius 0.15 to 0.45 m |
| Dynamic obstacles | 2 to 3, speed 0.20 to 0.40 m/s, radius 0.25 m |
| Checkpoints | 4, minimum separation 3 m |
| Time limit | 120 s |

Every generated arena is validated for reachability by breadth first search
before it is used, so an unwinnable arena cannot be drawn. The validation inflates
obstacles by the 0.22 m robot footprint radius plus a 0.15 m clearance margin, so
every route has genuine room for a robot with a body rather than merely being
passable by a point.

upwards, publicly, on the day.

## 4. The robot

The standard reference robot, unmodified. Wheel radius 0.050 m, separation
0.350 m, footprint radius 0.220 m, maximum linear velocity 0.50 m/s, maximum
angular velocity 1.80 rad/s, 360 beam LiDAR at 10 Hz with a range of 0.12 to
12.0 m.

You may not change the URDF, the sensor configuration, the controller limits or
the physics. Every team competes with identical hardware, and the entire
difference between entries is software. Modifying the robot is grounds for
disqualification from the leaderboard, though not from the academic mark.

## 5. Run protocol

Ambiguity in competition rules produces arguments, and arguments during a public
event are worse than rules that are slightly imperfect but stated in advance.

**Commissioning run.** Immediately before your scored attempts you receive **ten
unscored minutes** in the arena. You may teleoperate, run SLAM Toolbox, save a
map and check it. This mirrors how a commercial AMR is commissioned at a new
site, and it is why AMCL and everything in Lab 5 is directly relevant.

You may decline the commissioning run and navigate reactively. That is a
strategic choice, not a rule violation, and the Lab 10 numbers suggest it is a
poor one.

**Scored attempts.** Two attempts on two different seeds. Your better attempt
counts.

**Interventions.** An intervention is any physical or software action by a team
member after the start signal, including restarting a node, repositioning the
robot, or publishing anything by hand. Maximum two. The third ends the attempt at
its current score. The clock does not stop during an intervention.

**Stuck.** The robot is declared stuck after 20 seconds without 0.15 m of
displacement, measured as displacement from the oldest pose in the window, which
is the definition implemented by `StuckDetector` in the Lab 7 starter. A stuck
robot may only be recovered by an intervention.

**Blocked route.** One route between checkpoints will be blocked. You are not
told which, and it may be blocked from the start or during the run.

**Environment failures.** If `course-check` fails on the day, record the incident
ID and tell the organiser before your attempt. You will be reallocated a machine
and rescheduled within the session. No marks and no attempts are lost to
infrastructure.

## 6. Scoring

Computed by `arc_eval/scoring.py` and by nothing else, from the officially
recorded metrics JSON. A disputed score is resolved by re-running that function
on the recording, not by discussion.

```
score = max(0,
            100 x checkpoints_reached
          + 100 if all four reached
          + 100 x max(0, (120 - time_used) / 120)          if completed
          + 100 x min(1, optimal_path / actual_path)       if completed
          -  75 x static_collisions
          - 150 x dynamic_collisions
          -  20 x clearance_violations
          - 150 x interventions )
```

The optimal path length is computed by the same breadth first search used to
validate the arena, so the efficiency term is grounded rather than relative to
whichever team happened to do well.

Three properties of this formula are deliberate and worth understanding, because
they tell you what to optimise.

**The time and efficiency bonuses require completion.** A robot that gives up
immediately would otherwise collect the full time bonus for having used no time.

**Not hitting things beats being fast.** A clean run finishing in 110 seconds
scores 608. A run finishing in 40 seconds with two collisions scores 517. The
priority order encoded here is: complete the mission, then do not hit things,
then take an efficient route, then be quick.

![Scores computed by `arc_eval/scoring.py` for six representative runs.](docs/figures/project2_scoring.png){width=95%}


**Interventions never pay.** Even gaining the entire time bonus does not cover
the cost of one intervention. If your robot is struggling, letting it recover on
its own is always worth more than helping it.

**Clearance violation** means the minimum clearance fell below 0.10 m without a
collision, counted once per continuous event.

## 7. Academic marking

Separate from the leaderboard, and worth three times as much. A team can win the
competition and receive an average academic mark, and the reverse.

| Component | Marks | What earns them |
|-----------|-------|-----------------|
| System architecture and justification | 20 | A defensible design, with the reasoning stated and connected to evidence |
| ROS engineering | 15 | Package structure, launch, configuration discipline, tests, reproducibility |
| Algorithmic and methodological understanding | 20 | Depth on what you built, including the parts you configured rather than wrote |
| Experimentation and benchmarking | 20 | Seeded evaluation against a baseline, correct treatment of uncertainty |
| Report and presentation | 15 | Clarity, honest limitations, a diagram that helps |
| Individual viva | 10 | Understanding of your own system and of the diagnostic workflow |

Multiplied by your individual peer contribution factor, 0.8 to 1.1.

### The experimentation requirement

This is the section teams most often lose marks on, so it is specified exactly.

Your report must compare your entry against **at least one baseline**, over **at
least 30 seeds from the published grammar**, using `arc_eval`. The obvious
baseline is the unmodified Lab 6 Nav2 configuration. If your entry is a tuned
Nav2, the baseline is untuned Nav2.

Report mean and standard deviation for every metric, and the success rate with
its confidence interval. **If the difference between your entry and the baseline
is smaller than the standard deviation, report it as inconclusive.** Doing that
correctly earns full marks for method. Presenting a difference inside the noise
as an improvement does not, and it is the most common error in these reports.

You must also include **one failure analysis**: a seed where your system behaved
badly, the mechanism, and what you would change. A team reporting thirty
successes out of thirty has either not run thirty seeds or has not examined them.

### The deployment question

Your report must answer, in half a page: which system would you actually deploy,
and why? This continues the Lab 10 exercise and it must engage with your own
measurements, including verifiability and what happens when your system is wrong.

## 8. Deliverables

**Repository**, pushed by the deadline:

- Packages, launch files, configuration, and a single command that starts your
  entry
- `README.md` that lets an organiser clone, build and run it without asking you
  anything. This will be tested by doing exactly that.
- `AI_USE.md` declaring language model use: which tool, what was generated, what
  you changed. Declared use is permitted and unpenalised. Undeclared use is an
  academic integrity matter.
- Trained models and training scripts, if you used learning
- Your 30 seed evaluation JSON files and the configuration that produced them
- Tests

**Report**, six pages maximum excluding references and appendices, as PDF.

**Competition entry**, present on the day with a working system and a laptop-free
setup: everything runs from your repository on the provided machine.

**Individual viva**, five minutes per student during the event: one question from
the published bank, one justification of a parameter in your own submission, and
one live fault to diagnose aloud.

## 9. Awards

Separate from both the leaderboard and the marks, decided by the demonstrators.

- **Grand Champion**, highest leaderboard score
- **Best Navigation**, most efficient and reliable route execution
- **Best AI Solution**, most effective use of learning, whether or not it won
- **Most Robust Robot**, best recovery from things going wrong
- **Best Engineering Design**, cleanest system a stranger could pick up and run

The Best AI Solution award exists to make sure a team that tries a learned
approach honestly, measures it properly and reports that it did not beat the
classical baseline is recognised rather than penalised. That is a good piece of
engineering and the course should say so.

## 10. Schedule

**Week 9.** Read this document, the grammar and the scoring function. Decide your
approach and write down why. Run the Lab 6 baseline over 30 practice seeds so you
know what you are trying to beat.

**Week 10.** Build. If you are training a policy, train it this week, because
Lab 9 measured 1.9 minutes for 200,000 steps and you will want many more runs
than you expect.

**Week 11.** Evaluate over 30 seeds and iterate on what the results tell you.
Rehearse the commissioning run against practice seeds until saving and loading a
map takes under five minutes reliably.

**Week 12.** Rehearse a clean clone and build from your own README on a fresh
machine. Finish the report. Compete.

The two most common ways teams lose marks here are leaving the evaluation until
the last day, and discovering a missing dependency during the clean clone in
front of an organiser. Both are avoidable in an hour in week 11.

## 11. A note on what this is for

The competition is the memorable part and it is worth ten percent. The other
thirty percent is for building something defensible and understanding it, and
that is the part that transfers.

When you are asked in an interview what you have built, the useful answer is not
that your robot won a competition. It is that you built an autonomous navigation
system, measured it against a baseline over thirty seeds, found where it failed,
explained why, and can say what you would do differently. That answer is
available to every team here regardless of where they finish on the day.
